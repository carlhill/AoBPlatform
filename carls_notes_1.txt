## Three corrections before anything else

**1. "A point for each credential entered" is the fraud hole.** That's the one item on your list I'd reject outright. If typing an accreditation number scores a point, a fraudster types ten and clears any threshold. **Points must attach only to *verified* checks, never to entered data.** Entering an HPI-O = 0. An HPI-O confirmed with Services Australia = 3.

Two more consequences:
- **Weight by who did the verifying.** A third party (ABR, Commonwealth, a CA, an accrediting body) is worth far more than us eyeballing something.
- **Independence beats count.** Five signals all derived from one submitted document are one signal. Ten weak points must never equal one strong one, so the gate should be **two-part: a minimum score AND at least one strong check**. Otherwise 8 trivial points clears a threshold of 5.

And: **never show the applicant their score or the thresholds.** "You need 5 points, here's what scores" is a fraud playbook. Tell them what's *missing* ("please provide an HPI-O"), never where they sit.

**2. The billing dashboard is the best idea in your message — and we don't have the data.** Making the doctor the detector is genuinely strong: nobody has more motive to notice their provider number being misused. It's a real selling point.

But we hold **consent records, not claims**. The architecture puts claim lodgement out of frame. So we can honestly show *"every AoB signed in your name, across every practice on AoBPlatform"* — not *"everything Medicare paid against your number"*. That still catches platform-side fraud. It does **not** catch a practice billing Medicare directly without us.

If we pitch it as the latter, we're over-claiming. The real version needs the practitioner to authorise us through PRODA/HPOS — big build, big trust ask, roadmap not now.

Also: **7 years collides with our 2-year retention** (REQ-OFF-07). We can only show what we still hold. Extending to 7 is a deliberate retention decision with privacy consequences, not a reporting tweak.

**3. Reselling AHPRA lookups — licence first, economics second.** The maths works: one PIE account, 50k GPs ≈ $55k/yr, versus every practice paying $1 per doctor and paying again for the same doctor elsewhere. Pooling is genuinely cheaper for the market.

## The audit holes

Four writes left no trace. Fixed and verified against the live service:

| | Was | Now |
|---|---|---|
| `addLocation` | event only **if the address auto-validated** | always — the silent case was the common one |
| `addDepartment` | nothing | `department.added` |
| `addCredential` | nothing | `credential.added` |
| `removeCredential` | **hard `deleteMany`** | soft, with author and required reason |

The removal one mattered most. A credential is identity evidence, and it could vanish leaving no record it existed *and* none that anyone removed it. It also moved the identity score invisibly — the score counts *verified* credentials, so a deletion silently lowered a practice's standing between two reviews with nothing to explain the drop.

The reason is required because **"removed" answers nothing**: *entered twice*, *belongs to another practice*, and *turned out to be false* are three very different findings, and only the third says anything about the applicant.

**One thing worth knowing about that migration.** Uniqueness was on `(practiceId, credentialType, credentialValue)`, so a soft-deleted row would hold that slot forever and block re-adding something removed by mistake. It's now a partial index over live rows only — and the migration finds the old one **by shape, not by name**, because Prisma's generated name exceeds Postgres's 63-character limit and gets truncated *not where you'd guess* (it loses "lue" from the middle of the last column). My hand-written `DROP` matched nothing and reported success, leaving both indexes in place. I only caught it by listing the indexes afterwards.

On your design — agreed, with two additions I think matter more than the part you asked for.

Add the old address to the recipients. You said new address + group email. But the threat this defends against is the one already written into the review-task prompt: somebody redirecting where our messages go. If an attacker changes the address, the new address is theirs, and the group email may also be theirs if they're inside the practice. The old address is the only channel the person making the change doesn't control by having made it. It's why banks and Google send "your email was changed" to the previous address, not just the new one. And if the group email is changed in the same save, the notice has to go to the old group email too, or changing both at once defeats the control.

Don't revoke the passkeys until the new address is proven. Today the revocation is instant. That means one console session is enough to lock the real administrator out — takeover and denial-of-service in a single click, with no email to warn anyone. Holding the change pending verification means the legitimate admin keeps working and gets a "no, stop this" link.

That gives:

| On save | Record the change as requested, not applied. Old address still works, passkeys intact. |
| Verification → new address | "Prove you hold this." |
| Notice → old address + group email | "This was requested by X at 09:14. Not you? Stop it." |
| Review task | Already raised, high-stakes, no automated check may close it. Unchanged. |
| On verify | Apply, revoke old passkeys, send the enrolment link. |
| On "stop it", or 5 days' silence | Discard, and raise a high-stakes task either way. |

Five days matches the existing emailed-link expiry, so it's not a new number to remember.

The cost is one pending-change table and a state the application screen has to show ("waiting on confirmation from the new address"). The benefit is that an email change stops being a one-click account takeover.

Two things before I build it. This is an auth flow and it sends mail, which CLAUDE.md §7 says I ask about first — so tell me to go ahead. And confirm the pending-change behaviour is what you want, because it's a real behaviour change: an administrator changing their own address will no longer see it take effect immediately, and that will surprise people who expect a save to save.

A role can express	What's actually needed
"is an assignor"	"may act for this patient"
—	"until 14th birthday / revocation / date"
—	"revocable by the patient, visibly"

So the fix is a relationship record, not a role — (assignor, patient, basis, from, until, revokedAt, revokedBy) — with the same shape everything else here has: RLS keyed on it, so the database refuses rather than the query being careful. Three decisions I'd need from you before building it, and they're product decisions rather than technical ones:

Can a patient revoke an assignor themselves? I'd say yes and that it must be visible to them, or "family reporting" becomes one person's records exposed to another by whoever set it up.

Now your email-change question. One part of your proposal I'd argue against, and I think the third part is what resolves it.

Old-address-authorises is stronger, and it breaks the case the feature exists for. It defeats takeover completely — an attacker with a console session can't proceed without the old inbox. But the most common legitimate reason to change the address is that you've lost access to the old one: left the practice, mailbox closed, account deleted. Requiring it to authorise means exactly the people who most need to change it can't, and every one of them becomes a support ticket where somebody has to judge identity over the phone. That's a worse security position than the mechanism it replaced.

Which is why Google and Microsoft do the thing you said third. A backup address gives a second channel that isn't the one being changed — so you get the strength of "somebody who already had access must consent" without depending on an address that may be gone.

So I'd build it as:

| Set a backup address | Verified when set, and never the same as the primary — one inbox covering both is one compromise covering both |
| On change | New address confirms with a code; old and backup are told, with a link to stop it |
| If old is unreachable | Backup still carries the alarm, so the change is still witnessed |
| After it takes effect | Old and backup can still stop it for a cooling-off period — Google gives 7 days |

That keeps "somebody other than the requester must be able to object", without making an unreachable address a dead end.

Other things worth checking that you didn't list:

Rate-limit the changes. Three address changes in a month is itself a signal, whatever each one claimed.
Refuse an address already in use by another contact at the practice or another practitioner — one inbox for two identities means one person can confirm both.
Notify on completion, not just on request. The current design tells people it was asked for; the moment it takes effect is the one worth knowing about.
Don't revoke the practitioner's passkey on an email change. For the practice admin that's right, because the account belongs to the practice and changes hands. A practitioner's credential is theirs and doesn't move — revoking it would punish them for updating their address.

On SMS OTP later: worth knowing it's a weaker second factor than the passkey they already hold — SIM swap is the standard attack — so I'd treat it as a recovery channel, never as a step-up.

Want me to build it that way — backup address, new-confirms, old-and-backup-can-stop, with the cooling-off window? It's mostly a second caller into machinery that already exists.
